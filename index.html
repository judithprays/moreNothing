Principles
Situation

Our team was given a request to integrate a bespoke, vibecoded landing page into our infrastructure for a GTM launch of a new internal product — to be completed, at engineering's end, by next week. Several key stakeholders were out of office, and questions were being answered piecemeal across Slack threads, with no clear owner making decisions.

Challenge

Before we could even discuss implementation, we needed clarity on who actually had the authority to answer product questions and make tradeoffs.

Move

I identified the people who could speak for the product and engineering decisions and pulled everyone into a single meeting. Once we had the right people together, the product representative stated that the landing page should only be visible to US visitors.

That immediately struck me as odd. Limit viewership on a marketing page? When EU is 60% of our subscriber base?

The engineering discussion shifted toward how we would implement the restriction, but I stopped the conversation and asked a different question:

"Why is this a requirement?"

No one knew.

Thinking through what I did know, I remembered that the marketing team had been struggling with attribution and that GDPR affects tracking differently in Europe. I wondered if the real requirement was related to campaign measurement rather than page visibility.

After digging further, we confirmed that the paid campaign was only targeting the US, but the landing page itself didn't need to be restricted.

The requirement was removed.

Result
  • Turned fragmented conversations into a single decision-making forum.
  • Eliminated unnecessary implementation complexity.
  • Prevented the team from building the wrong solution.
  • Clarified the business goal instead of simply executing the stated requirement.
Leadership Principle

Create clarity before creating software.

Find the smallest version of the problem that creates meaningful progress.
Avoiding the Russian Winter
Situation

I was asked to create a demo for an engineering all-hands. The only direction I was given was the time limit, so I had complete freedom to decide what to share.

One of my biggest strengths as a leader has always been reducing scope. I joke that I'm most famous at work for doing nothing — some of my biggest wins have come from identifying work we didn't need to do, removing complexity, or shrinking projects down to their most valuable pieces.

Challenge

I wanted to use the all-hands opportunity to share a principle that I had seen repeatedly impact our team's ability to execute: our tendency to underestimate complexity.

When teams take on something too large, they often enter what I called the "Russian Winter" — a reference to Napoleon's failed invasion of Russia, where an ambitious plan underestimated the realities of distance, logistics, and complexity.

Move

I created a talk about the importance of breaking work down into the smallest possible pieces.

The goal wasn't just better estimation. It was changing how we approached problems:

  • What is the smallest version of this?
  • What can we learn sooner?
  • What can we remove?
  • What is the actual outcome we need?

The idea resonated with the team and became a shared language. "Russian Winter" became a phrase people used when projects started becoming too complex. Someone even made a T-shirt.

Result
  • Created a shared engineering principle around reducing complexity.
  • Changed team conversations from "How do we accomplish all of this?" to "What is the smallest thing that gets us closer?"
  • Helped teams avoid overcommitting and made execution more predictable.
Leadership Principle

Great leaders don't just help teams do more. They help teams identify what matters most and make progress one achievable step at a time.

Situation

About six months into managing a new team, I inherited an engineer I had worked with years earlier. Back then, she had been an exceptional QA lead — organized, respected, and a natural leader.

After QA was eliminated, she had been moved into a junior software engineering role. She spent a year on a highly technical backend team where she struggled to find context, ownership, or opportunities to contribute in ways that played to her strengths. By the time she joined my team, her confidence was gone.

Challenge

I remembered a different version of her.

Rather than focusing only on the gaps in her technical skills, I wanted to create an environment where her strengths could re-emerge while she built those skills.

Move

I started her with approachable technical work and paired her with teammates who enjoyed mentoring. As she gained confidence, I intentionally looked for opportunities that required leadership, coordination, and ownership.

When we needed to migrate 29 landing pages from Instapage into our Sanity page builder — a project involving 18 stakeholders across the organization — I asked her to lead it.

She owned the communication, coordination, and execution from start to finish.

Result
  • The migration was completed smoothly with almost no intervention from me.
  • She received public recognition from my manager and our CEO for how well this went.
  • She became eligible for promotion.
  • More importantly, she rediscovered her confidence as a leader.
Leadership Principle

My dad used to say — don't bang the door down, just find the key. Everyone can be a star, they just need to be unleashed on the right problem in the right environment.

Situation

In 2026, adopting AI became a major initiative for our organization. Engineers had very different reactions — some were excited, while others had genuine fear and skepticism about what AI meant for their roles and the future of engineering.

I had one particularly senior engineer on my team who was very vocal and very anti-AI. Every one-on-one was filled with dread, public Slack messages were "suicidal level dark (but 'just a joke haha')," and many project conversations had a consistent voice of negativity.

Challenge

I needed to address the impact of her communication without dismissing the value of her perspective.

She was one of the most senior engineers on the team, and her skepticism was actually valuable. Healthy skepticism protects teams from blindly adopting trends and helps surface risks others may miss.

The goal was not to make her enthusiastic about AI or remove disagreement. The goal was to help her channel her perspective in a way that moved the team forward.

Move

I gave direct feedback that her voice carried significant influence and that she needed to be intentional about how she used it. I shared specific examples where her comments, even when intended humorously, could have a stronger impact than she realized.

At the same time, I made it clear that she was not expected to adopt AI in any particular way. I wanted her to explore it authentically and develop her own perspective.

I also noticed she was becoming isolated. AI experimentation had a tendency to send people into individual "caves," so I intentionally paired her with engineers who were excited about AI and whom she respected.

Through those conversations, she began seeing practical examples of how AI could enhance the work she already valued.

She especially thrived when we needed to take a vibe-coded landing page and turn it into production-quality infrastructure. She became my go-to person for transforming AI-generated prototypes into maintainable software. I gave her the title "Top Slop Chef," which she loved so much that she made it her official badge.

Result
  • Maintained a culture where skepticism and disagreement remained welcome.
  • Helped the team move through a major technology transition without losing an important voice.
  • Transformed a source of tension into a source of expertise.
  • Helped a senior engineer find her own authentic way to engage with AI.
Leadership Principle

Embrace and channel. Great leaders don't eliminate strong perspectives — they help people use their strengths in service of the team.

Situation

Our team had built a self-service landing page builder that was becoming a core part of our portfolio. At an offsite, a marketing leader demonstrated a vibe-coded landing page they had built instead. My team walked away discouraged, questioning whether our work still mattered.

As I talked to people, I realized the concern extended beyond engineering. Designers disliked the quality of the AI-generated experience, and marketing partners still preferred the performance and reliability of our platform. The issue wasn't that our product had lost value — it was that we weren't staying visible.

Challenge

I could have tried to reassure the team that our work still mattered.

Instead, I wanted to prove it.

Move

I looked for the next opportunity to create a success story.

I used Claude to monitor public Slack channels for new landing page requests. Within a week, I found a backend team building an Anthropic MCP integration that needed a GTM landing page.

I reached out, introduced the page builder, and within a day we had a draft ready on our platform.

Result
  • The partner team launched faster.
  • My team saw immediate evidence that their work was valuable.
  • The page builder gained momentum and became the default solution for landing pages across the organization.
Leadership Principle

When confidence is shaken, don't give a speech — create a win.

Situation

As an IC, every engineer rotated into the role of Desktop Release Captain every few months. The release process relied on a ~20-page document written years earlier. Because the rotation was so infrequent, no one developed muscle memory, and every engineer approached their turn with a sense of dread.

Challenge

During one of my rotations, I accidentally released the wrong branch, resulting in the wrong desktop version being shipped. Rolling it back cost the company thousands of dollars.

It was the most public and expensive mistake of my career.

Move
How did the system allow a reasonable person to make this mistake?

I reviewed the release documentation and found that the critical step was ambiguous. Rather than quietly editing the document myself, I invited the original author — a principal engineer — and the rest of the engineering team to a working session.

Together we walked through the process step by step, rewriting unclear instructions, removing unnecessary complexity, and identifying opportunities to automate parts of the release process.

The principal engineer was genuinely appreciative. The process had become second nature to him, and he hadn't realized how difficult it was for everyone else.

Result
  • Simplified and clarified the release process.
  • Reduced cognitive load for future release captains.
  • Automated portions of the workflow.
  • Eliminated future desktop release incidents.
Leadership Principle

Treat mistakes as evidence of a system that can be improved.
