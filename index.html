Principles
Situation

In the summer of 2026, our team was given a request to integrate a bespoke, vibecoded landing page into our infrastructure for Top Priority GTM launch of a new internal product — to be completed, of course, by next week. It was highly political from the top and highly emotional from the bottom, with several key stakeholders out of office, and questions were being answered piecemeal across Slack threads, with no clear owner making decisions.

Challenge

Before we could even discuss implementation, we needed clarity on who actually had the authority to speak to tradeoffs so we could get this executed on time and without revisions.

Move

I wrangled official representatives to speak for the product and design decisions and pulled everyone into a single meeting. Once we had the right people together and the high level strategy decided and communicated - we began discussing requirements. The product representative stated that the landing page should only be visible to US visitors.

That struck me as odd. Limit viewership on a marketing page? When EU is 60% of our subscriber base?

Given the pace and the tension, it wasn't easy, but I stopped the conversation to ask:

"Why is this a requirement?"

No one could back it up.

Thinking through what I did know, I remembered that the marketing team had been struggling with attribution and that GDPR is stricter in Europe. I wondered if the requirement was related to campaign measurement, the marketing team's success metric, rather than page visibility, the product team's success metric.

After digging further, we confirmed that the paid campaign was only targeting the US, but the landing page itself didn't need to be restricted.

The requirement was removed.

Result
  • Turned fragmented conversations and lack of clarity into an effective decision-making forum.
  • Eliminated unnecessary implementation complexity.
  • Prevented the team from building the wrong solution.
  • Dodged a significant decrease in views, the entire goal of a marketing page.
  • Delivered page on time and at engineering standard.
Situation

I was asked to create a demo for an engineering all-hands. There was no direction, only a time constraint.

Challenge

Even though it wasn't exactly the assignment, I wanted to use the all-hands opportunity to share a principle that I had seen repeatedly impact our team's ability to execute: our tendency to chunk work too large, and then get caught by surprise complexity. I also wanted it to be fun, because an hour of demos from the eng team can get a bit... well, you know.

Move

I created a talk about the importance of breaking work down into the smallest possible pieces. I wanted to start conversations around:

  • What is the smallest version of this?
  • What can we learn sooner?
  • What can we remove?
  • What is the actual outcome we need?

The idea resonated with the team and became a shared language. "Russian Winter" became a phrase people used when projects revealed hidden complexities. One of the principal engineers requested this be the official engineering team sweater.

Results
  • Created a shared engineering principle around breaking work down.
  • Helped teams avoid overcommitting and made execution more predictable.
Situation

About six months into managing a new team, I inherited an engineer I had worked with years earlier. Back then, she had been an exceptional QA lead — organized, respected and a natural leader.

After QA was eliminated, she had been moved into a junior software engineering role. She spent a year on a highly technical backend team where she struggled to find context, ownership, or opportunities to contribute in ways that played to her strengths. By the time she joined my team, her confidence was gone.

Challenge

I remembered a different version of her.

Rather than focusing only on the gaps in her technical skills, I wanted to create an environment where her strengths could shine to give her more momentum to rebuild.

Move

I started her with approachable technical work and paired her with teammates who enjoyed mentoring. As she gained confidence, I intentionally looked for opportunities that required leadership, coordination, and ownership.

When we needed to migrate 29 landing pages from Instapage into our Sanity page builder — a project involving 18 stakeholders across the organization — I asked her to lead it.

She owned the communication, coordination, and execution from start to finish.

Results
  • The migration was completed smoothly, ahead of schedule, all stakeholders thrilled - with almost no intervention from me.
  • She received public recognition from my manager and our CEO for how well this went.
  • She became eligible for promotion.
  • Her morale sliders were 10/10 and she was glowing throughout the whole project.
  • More importantly, she rediscovered her confidence as a leader.
Situation

In 2026, adopting AI became a major initiative for our organization. Engineers had very different reactions — some were excited, while others had genuine fear and skepticism about what AI meant for their roles and the future of engineering.

I had one particularly senior engineer on my team who was very vocal and very anti-AI. Every one-on-one was filled with dread, public Slack messages were suicidal-level-dark (but 'just a joke haha')," and project conversations had a consistent voice of negativity.

Challenge

I needed to address the impact of her communication without dismissing the value of her perspective.

She was the most senior engineers on the team, and her perspective was valuable. Healthy skepticism protects teams from blindly adopting trends and surfaces risks others may gloss over.

The goal was not to make her an AI champion or remove her voice. The goal was to help her channel her perspective in a way that moved the team forward, rather than have us wondering if we should be calling the suicide prevention hotline.

Move

I gave direct feedback that her voice carried significant influence and that she needed to be intentional about how she used it. I shared specific examples where her comments, even when intended humorously, could have a stronger impact than she realized.

At the same time, I made it clear that she was not expected to adopt AI in any specific way. I wanted her to explore it authentically and develop her own perspective, and reinstated the value of her experience.

I also noticed she was becoming isolated. AI experimentation had a tendency to send people into individual "caves," so I intentionally paired her with engineers who were excited about AI and whom she respected.

Through those conversations, she began seeing practical examples of how AI could enhance the work she already valued.

She especially thrived in situations when we needed to take vibe-coded work and turn it into production-quality infrastructure. She became my go-to person for transforming AI-generated prototypes into maintainable software. I gave her the title "Top Slop Chef," which she loved so much that she made it her official badge.

Result
  • Maintained an inclusive culture where healthy conflict can yield positive outcomes.
  • Helped the team move through a major technology transition without losing an important voice.
  • Transformed a source of tension into a source of expertise.
  • Helped a senior engineer find her own authentic way to engage with AI.
Situation

Our team had built a self-service landing page builder that was becoming a core part of our portfolio. At an offsite, a marketing leader demonstrated a vibe-coded landing page they had built instead - which seemed like a major side step to our team's work. My team walked away discouraged, questioning whether our work still mattered.

As I talked to cross functional partners, I realized the concern extended beyond engineering. Designers disliked the quality of the AI-generated experience and lack of consistency with our design language, and marketing partners still preferred the performance and reliability of our platform. The issue wasn't that our product had lost value — it was that the org wasn't fully aware of all the benefits of what we were building.

Challenge

I could have tried to reassure the team that our work still mattered.

Instead, I wanted to prove it.

Move

I looked for the next opportunity to create a success story.

I used Claude to monitor public Slack channels for new landing page requests. Within a week, I found a backend team building an Anthropic MCP integration that needed a GTM landing page.

I reached out, introduced the page builder, and within a day we had a draft ready on our platform. The team was thrilled that the task was complete practically effortlessly and within our infrastructure, and we got to use our tool for this highly visable project.

Result
  • The partner team launched faster.
  • My team saw immediate evidence that their work was valuable.
  • The page builder gained momentum and became the default solution for landing pages across the organization.
  • Our team unilaterally became go to experts on how the org should be creating landing pages
Situation

As an IC, every engineer rotated into the role of Desktop Release Captain every few months. The release process relied on a ~20-page document written 5 years earlier. Because the rotation was so infrequent, no one developed muscle memory, and every engineer approached their turn with a sense of dread.

Challenge

During one of my rotations, I accidentally released the wrong branch, resulting in the wrong desktop version being shipped. Rolling it back cost the company thousands of dollars.

It was the most public and expensive mistake of my career.

Move

I immediately had to understand how I could have done this. I reviewed the release documentation and found that the critical step was ambiguous. Rather than quietly editing the document myself or resolving this in DMs, I invited the original author — a principal engineer who had been there for more than a decade — and the rest of the engineering team to a working session to improve this document.

Together we walked through each line of the process, rewriting unclear instructions, removing unnecessary complexity, and identifying opportunities to automate parts of the release process.

The principal engineer was genuinely appreciative. The process had become second nature to him, and he hadn't realized how difficult it was for everyone else.

Result
  • Simplified and clarified the entire release process.
  • Reduced cognitive load for future release captains.
  • Automated portions of the workflow.
  • Eliminated future desktop release incidents.
